# Request for a second opinion on the word problems in a 7th-grade weekly-check bank

## Who we are and what this is
Two 7th-grade math teachers at a Florida middle school (on-level classes, Florida B.E.S.T. standards, MA.7 benchmarks) give a common 5-question check every week on the benchmark taught that week, as a self-grading Google Form. It is part of a formal professional-development plan, so the data matters: after each check we reteach the weakest skill and give a 3-question re-check; a 2-question "review" form on an earlier benchmark is given alongside it as a no-reteach control. The gain from check to re-check, measured against the control, is the number the whole plan reports.

Every item is auto-scored: multiple choice (4 options), checkboxes (all-or-nothing), or a typed number (a whole number, decimal, fraction a/b, or mixed number a b/c; no units, $, %, or π). Students work on paper and enter only the answer. After submitting, each student sees the worked solution and the common mistakes behind each wrong answer.

The bank has already had two rounds of review: every key is recomputed, every wrong choice is tied to a named student error, and each item was checked against the benchmark's official text and test item specifications (FLDOE). What we have NOT had is a fresh read of the word problems as stories: are they clear, realistic and fair for a 12-year-old reading on their own, and does the math genuinely match the situation?

## What we are asking you to do
Read every word problem below. For each one, judge:
1. Clarity and reading load: could an on-level 7th grader (some reading below grade level, some English learners) understand what is being asked without help? Flag unnecessary words, unfamiliar vocabulary, unclear referents, or a question buried at the end.
2. Realism: are the numbers and situation believable (prices, speeds, sizes, ages, quantities)? Anything that would make a kid stop and say "that's not how that works"?
3. Story-to-math match: does the situation actually call for the operation the key uses? Any hidden assumption (rounding direction, whether tax applies, inclusive counting, units) that a careful student could reasonably take the other way?
4. Fairness: cultural or background knowledge a student might not have (specific sports, foods, products, regional terms), stereotyped names or roles, or a context that could land badly.
5. Distractors (multiple choice): is each wrong choice a believable student answer, and is the correct one not identifiable by form alone?
6. Typed-answer items: is the expected form unambiguous given the typing rule, and is there any second reasonable answer (for example, a different rounding path)?

## How to answer
Keep the math the same unless you find an actual flaw; we are after wording, realism and fairness, not new problems. For every item give one line: the item ID, then OK or a specific rewrite of the stem (and of options if needed), in this format so we can apply it directly:

`MA.7.AR.2.2/chk/4 | REWRITE | <new stem text> ||| <option A> | <option B> | <option C> | <option D>`  (omit the options part if unchanged)
`MA.7.AR.2.2/chk/1 | OK`

Notation we use: minus is −, multiplication ×, fractions like 3/4, mixed numbers like 2 1/4, money with $ and two decimals. Keep stems to one or two short paragraphs. Do not add information that changes the answer. At the end, list any patterns you noticed across the bank (3 to 6 bullets).

## Form types
- chk = Check (5 questions, the week the benchmark is taught; Q5 is usually the "rigor" item meant to reach the scale's top level)
- re = Re-check (3 questions, after the reteach; should be as hard as the check)
- cmp = Review (2 questions, the control; should match the check's difficulty)

### MA.7.DP.2.1: Determine the sample space for a simple experiment.

**MA.7.DP.2.1/chk/0** (Check Q1; calculator allowed; multiple choice)

Each letter of the word DIFFERENCE is written on its own card, one card for each letter, and the cards are placed in a jar. One card is drawn at random and its letter is recorded.

How many outcomes are in the sample space for this experiment?

- A. 8
- B. 7
- C. 10
- D. 6

Key: B  
Solution: DIFFERENCE has 10 letters, so there are 10 cards, but F is on 2 cards and E is on 3 cards. / The outcome is the letter that is recorded, and a letter that is on more than one card is still one outcome. / The sample space is {D, I, F, E, R, N, C}, which has 7 outcomes, not 10.

**MA.7.DP.2.1/chk/1** (Check Q2; calculator allowed; multiple choice)

A fair 6-sided die has faces numbered 1 through 6. The die is rolled once and the number on the top face is recorded.

What is the sample space for this experiment?

- A. {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
- B. {6}
- C. {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}
- D. {even, odd}

Key: A  
Solution: The sample space lists every outcome that can be recorded. / The die can show 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, so the sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. / 6 is only the number of outcomes, and 'even' and 'odd' are events made of several outcomes, not outcomes themselves.

**MA.7.DP.2.1/chk/2** (Check Q3; calculator allowed; multiple choice)

Experiment 1: A spinner has 5 equal sections colored red, green, orange, purple and red. It is spun once and the color it lands on is recorded.
Experiment 2: A bag holds 1 orange, 3 green and 2 red marbles. One marble is drawn at random and its color is recorded.

Which statement about the sample spaces of the two experiments is true?

- A. The sample space for the spinner has one outcome that the sample space for the bag does not have.
- B. The sample space for the spinner has 5 outcomes, and the sample space for the bag has 6 outcomes.
- C. The two experiments have the same sample space, {orange, green, red, purple}.
- D. The sample space for the bag has one outcome that the sample space for the spinner does not have.

Key: A  
Solution: Each outcome is a color, and a color is listed once no matter how many sections or marbles have it. / Spinner: {orange, green, red, purple}. Bag: {orange, green, red}. / The spinner can land on purple, and the bag has no purple marble, so the sample space for the spinner has one more outcome.

**MA.7.DP.2.1/chk/3** (Check Q4; calculator allowed; checkboxes)

- C. queen of hearts
- D. 7 of spades

Solution: Each of the 13 hearts is one outcome: ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, jack, queen and king of hearts. / The 7 of spades is not one of the 13 hearts, so it cannot be drawn. / 'Red' is a color every card in this experiment has; it is not a card, so it is not an outcome.

**MA.7.DP.2.1/chk/4** (Check Q5, rigor item; calculator allowed; checkboxes)

Select all the experiments whose sample space is {purple, green, blue}.

- C. Drawing a marble from a bag that holds 3 purple, 5 green and 4 blue marbles
- D. Spinning a spinner whose 4 equal sections are green, purple, green, purple
- E. Drawing a marble from a bag that holds 3 purple, 5 green, 4 blue and 4 yellow marbles

### MA.7.NSO.1.2: Rewrite rational numbers in different but equivalent forms including fractions, mixed numbers, repeating decimals and percentages to solve mathematical and real-world problems.

**MA.7.NSO.1.2/chk/2** (Check Q3; no calculator; multiple choice)

Two identical bottles of sports drink are partly full. The first bottle is 10/11 full and the second bottle is 95% full.

The first bottle holds ____ the second bottle.

- A. more than
- B. less than
- C. the same amount as
- D. cannot be compared without knowing how much a bottle holds

Key: B  
Solution: Rewrite both amounts in the same form. 10/11 = 10 ÷ 11 = 0.90 (repeating), which is about 90.9%. / 90.9% is less than 95%, so the first bottle holds less than the second bottle.

**MA.7.NSO.1.2/chk/4** (Check Q5, rigor item; no calculator; multiple choice)

Maya uses a calculator to find 190/33. The calculator shows 5.757575758. Maya says that 190/33 = 5.757575758.

Is Maya correct? Choose the statement that is true.

- A. No. The decimal never ends, so 190/33 is not a rational number and has no exact value.
- B. Yes. 190/33 is a rational number, so its decimal must end, and 5.757575758 is where it ends.
- C. No. 190/33 = 5.75 (repeating), a repeating decimal. The calculator rounded the last digit it could show.
- D. No. The calculator cut off the digits it could not show, so the exact value is 5.757575757, with no more digits after that.

Key: C  
Solution: 190 ÷ 33 = 5.757575…; the block 75 repeats forever, so the exact value is 5.75 (repeating). / The display holds 9 decimal places. The tenth digit is 5, so the ninth digit 7 rounds up to 8. The display is rounded, not exact. / A fraction of two integers is rational even when its decimal repeats, so Maya is not correct.

**MA.7.NSO.1.2/re/0** (Re-check Q1; no calculator; multiple choice)

Two identical bottles of sports drink are partly full. The first bottle is 2/3 full and the second bottle is 66.6 (repeating)% full.

The first bottle holds ____ the second bottle.

- B. cannot be compared without knowing how much a bottle holds
- C. the same amount as
- D. more than

Key: C

- B. the same amount as

**MA.7.NSO.1.2/cmp/1** (Review Q2; no calculator; multiple choice)

A recipe needs 2/3 cup of sugar. The only scoop in the kitchen holds 0.3 cup. Mia needs to know how many scoops to use.

Which statement is correct?

- A. Rewrite 0.3 as 3/10. Then 3/10 ÷ 2/3 = 9/20, so she needs less than 1 scoop.
- B. Rewrite 2/3 as 0.66. Then 0.66 ÷ 0.3 = 2.2, so she needs exactly 2.2 scoops.
- C. Rewrite 0.3 as 3/10. Then 2/3 ÷ 3/10 = 2 2/9, so she needs a little more than 2 scoops.
- D. Rewrite 0.3 as 1/3. Then 2/3 ÷ 1/3 = 2, so she needs exactly 2 scoops.

Key: C  
Solution: 0.3 is a terminating decimal, so write it as the fraction 3/10. 2/3 is a repeating decimal (0.6 (repeating)), so keep it as a fraction. / 2/3 ÷ 3/10 = 2/3 × 10/3 = 20/9 = 2 2/9. / Mia needs 2 2/9 scoops, a little more than 2 scoops. Rounding 2/3 to 0.66 gives 2.2, which is close but not exact.


### MA.7.DP.2.2: Given the probability of a chance event, interpret the likelihood of it occurring. Compare the probabilities of chance events.

**MA.7.DP.2.2/chk/0** (Check Q1; calculator allowed; multiple choice)

Four weather forecasts give these chances of rain tomorrow in four towns: 0.09, 4/5, 52%, 0.97.

Which chance describes a town where rain is almost certain?

- A. 4/5
- B. 52%
- C. 0.97
- D. 0.09

Key: C  
Solution: An event is almost certain when its probability is very close to 1 (100%). / 0.97 is 3 hundredths from 1, so that event is almost certain. / 4/5 = 0.8 and 52% = 0.52 are likely but not almost certain; 0.09 is close to 0, so that event is unlikely.

### MA.7.DP.2.4: Use a simulation of a simple experiment to find experimental probabilities and compare them to theoretical probabilities.

**MA.7.DP.2.4/chk/0** (Check Q1; calculator allowed; multiple choice)

A fair 12-sided die is rolled 300 times. It lands on a 9 exactly 24 times.

Find the experimental probability of rolling a 9 and the theoretical probability of rolling a 9, each as a fraction in simplest form. Then compare them.

Which choice is correct?

- A. experimental 2/23, theoretical 1/12; the experimental probability is greater
- B. experimental 2/25, theoretical 1/12; the experimental probability is greater
- C. experimental 2/25, theoretical 1/12; the experimental probability is less
- D. experimental 1/12, theoretical 2/25; the experimental probability is greater

Key: C  
Solution: Experimental: 24 of the 300 rolls landed on 9, so 24/300 = 2/25. / Theoretical: 1 of the 12 equally likely faces is a 9, so 1/12. / 2/25 = 0.08 and 1/12 ≈ 0.083, so the experimental probability is slightly less than the theoretical probability. / 2/23 divides by the 276 rolls that were not a 9 instead of by all 300 rolls.

### MA.7.DP.1.5: Given a real-world numerical or categorical data set, choose and create an appropriate graphical representation.

**MA.7.DP.1.5/chk/0** (Check Q1; calculator allowed; multiple choice)

A student recorded the 280 kilograms of material collected at a recycling drive.

Paper Plastic Glass Metal 
126 70 56 28 


The student wants a display that answers this question at a glance: is Paper more than half of the total? Which display is the most appropriate?

- A. a bar chart
- B. a histogram
- C. a line plot
- D. a circle graph

Key: D  
Solution: The question compares one category with the whole. In a circle graph each section is the same fraction of the circle as its category is of the total, so a section can be compared with half of the circle at a glance (Paper is 45%). / A bar chart shows the amounts but not how each compares with the total; a histogram and a line plot are for numerical data.
